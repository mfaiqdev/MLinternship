Abstract

This study asks whether observed search and engagement signals can help prioritize content items for possible refresh review. Using the FlyRank pseudonymized warehouse and a March 2026 development window, I compared a transparent impressions/clicks baseline with a Random Forest using independent ranking and engagement features. The Week-4 baseline measured precision 0.768, recall 1.000, and F1 0.868, while the Week-5 Random Forest measured precision 0.309, recall 0.693, and F1 0.428 on the same client-grouped test set. A controlled Week-6 validation produced recall 0.973, precision 0.122, and F1 0.217, so the measured evidence does not show that the evaluated ML approach outperforms the baseline. The resulting action playbook is therefore framed as directional decision-support for human review, not as proof that a page needs a refresh or that a refresh will improve performance.

Introduction / Problem statement

Content teams can have many pages competing for limited refresh time. A useful system should help answer a practical question: which content should a reviewer inspect first, and why?

The initial research direction was the Refresh / Content Opportunity Scoring lane. The work began with a smaller starter dataset and a practical decision-support question, then moved to the larger pseudonymized warehouse for the later internship analysis.

Research question

Can observable search and engagement signals provide a useful ranking signal for prioritizing content items for human refresh review, and does a machine-learning approach outperform a transparent rule-based baseline?

Decision and cost of error

The intended user is a content strategist, SEO specialist, or reviewer deciding which pages deserve investigation first. A false positive can waste limited review time on a page that does not need attention. A false negative can cause a potentially valuable opportunity to be overlooked. The objective is therefore prioritization, not autonomous editorial decision-making.

What this study does and does not claim

  • It evaluates observed relationships within the available pseudonymized data.
  • It tests whether the evaluated ML approach improves on a transparent baseline under a client-grouped validation design.
  • It produces directional review priorities that can support human investigation.
  • It does not predict Google's ranking algorithm or prove why rankings changed.
  • It does not prove that a page is low quality or that refreshing it will improve traffic, rankings, clicks, or conversions.

Warehouse date context

The available warehouse context spans content creation dates from approximately October 16, 2024 through July 6, 2026, while the daily performance reporting window extends from January 27, 2025 through June 30, 2026. The experiment itself is restricted to March 2026. This distinction matters: the broader warehouse range describes the available source data, while March is the actual development window used for the reported model comparison.

Why March 2026?

March was selected as a middle development window rather than treating the latest available period as the modeling window. This reduces the temptation to tune against the newest observations and leaves later periods conceptually available for future out-of-time evaluation. This study does not claim that March results generalize to all future periods.

What was excluded from the public artifact

The public paper does not expose client names, client-specific URLs, raw search queries, private identifiers, raw page-level exports, credentials, or other potentially sensitive records. The repository's public-facing artifacts contain aggregated methodology and results rather than private production observations.

Analytics-unavailable observations are not automatically interpreted as zero activity. Missingness is treated as a measurement limitation rather than evidence that a page had no users or engagement.

Public-safety principle: the paper describes the dataset, experiment, metrics, and methodology at a level sufficient for inspection without publishing private client-level information.

Methodology

1. Unit of analysis and assumptions

  • Each observation represents one content item for one client on one reporting date.
  • The constructed label is treated as a proxy for a review condition, not as human-confirmed ground truth.
  • Search visibility and engagement are treated as observed signals rather than causal explanations.
  • Client grouping is used to reduce leakage across the same client's observations and to test whether patterns transfer across clients.
  • Model output is interpreted as prioritization evidence rather than an instruction to edit or publish content.

2. Transparent baseline

The Week-4 baseline flags a possible opportunity when:

gsc_impressions > 100 AND gsc_clicks <= 1

The reasoning is straightforward: a page with meaningful observed search visibility but extremely low click volume deserves inspection. The rule is intentionally transparent and serves as the benchmark against which the ML approach is evaluated. It is a decision aid, not ground truth.

3. Proxy label

The Week-5 modeling task constructed the following proxy label:

(gsc_impressions > 100) AND (gsc_clicks <= 1) AND (ga4_sessions == 0)

The March dataset contained 388,493 positive observations and 9,452,885 negative observations, giving a positive rate of approximately 3.95%. This imbalance is important when interpreting precision and recall.

The label is not a human judgment that content requires a refresh. It is a constructed proxy reflecting a specific combination of observed search visibility, click activity, and analytics availability.

4. Leakage correction

An early modeling attempt included variables that were directly involved in defining the target, including impressions, clicks, and sessions. Such a setup risks allowing the model to reproduce the labeling rule rather than learn an independent signal. The final feature set therefore removed those direct label components and retained ranking and engagement variables that were independent of the label formula.

This correction is an important part of the research process: the final comparison is based on the leakage-audited feature set rather than the initial formulation.

5. Model

The Week-5 model was a RandomForestClassifier with 100 trees, random_state=42, and class_weight="balanced". The model was trained using the five independent features listed in the Data section.

6. Validation design

The train/test split was grouped by client_hash_id using GroupShuffleSplit. The resulting split contained:

8,935,676
Training rows

44 training clients

905,702

11 held-out clients

There was zero client overlap between training and test sets. This prevents observations from the same client appearing on both sides of the split.

7. Week-6 validation audit

Week 6 kept the complete test set while using a 500,000-row training sample to avoid the memory constraints encountered during full-scale retraining. The validation Random Forest used 30 trees with random_state=42, class_weight="balanced", n_jobs=2, max_depth=12, and min_samples_leaf=10.

The validation was intended to audit the Week-5 conclusion rather than create a new claim of production performance.

8. Leakage checks

  • Direct feature/label column overlap: none.
  • Client overlap between training and test sets: none.
  • Proxy label explicitly documented rather than treated as ground truth.
  • Features used for the final model do not directly reproduce the label formula.
Interpretation: the leakage audit supports the validity of the evaluated setup, but it does not turn the proxy label into ground truth or establish that the model will generalize to future time periods.

Feature importance

The strongest measured Week-5 Random Forest feature was gsc_avg_position, accounting for approximately 96.24% of the model's feature importance under that fitted model.

Feature importance describes predictive contribution within this experiment. It is not evidence that ranking position causes low clicks, nor does it prove that changing ranking position will produce a particular outcome.

What the results mean

Measured finding: the transparent baseline currently provides stronger overall prioritization under the evaluated F1 metric than the Random Forest approaches tested here.

This is not a failure of the research. The experiment answered the comparison question honestly: the evaluated ML approach did not improve on the simpler baseline. That result is itself useful because it establishes that added model complexity was not justified by the measured performance in this setup.

Limitations & honest framing

  • Proxy target: the refresh label is constructed from observed signals and is not human-confirmed ground truth.
  • Single development window: the main analysis uses March 2026, so the findings may not generalize across time.
  • Client generalization: the grouped split tests held-out clients, but the experiment still covers only the clients represented in this release.
  • Validation sampling: ML-09 used a 500,000-row training sample for memory-efficient revalidation rather than an identical full-scale retraining.
  • Observational data: relationships between signals and labels do not establish causation.
  • Measurement availability: missing analytics can affect observed engagement signals and therefore the resulting prioritization.
  • Evaluation target: a strong F1 against a constructed proxy does not prove that a human reviewer would make a better editorial decision.
  • No intervention test: the study does not measure whether refreshing the recommended pages actually improves search or business outcomes.
  • Not autonomous: the output is a review queue, not a system for automatically rewriting, publishing, deleting, redirecting, or changing pages.

What would strengthen the research next

  1. Use human-reviewed refresh outcomes as a stronger target.
  2. Evaluate later time periods as a genuine out-of-time test.
  3. Compare additional simple ranking rules before increasing model complexity.
  4. Measure whether recommended refreshes lead to improved outcomes through a controlled intervention or longitudinal evaluation.

Ranked recommendations

The final action playbook translates the observed signals into a human-review queue. These recommendations are deliberately ranked by practical review value rather than presented as automated decisions.

1

HIGH_VISIBILITY_LOW_CLICKS

Review pages with more than 100 impressions and no more than 1 click first. Inspect title and snippet relevance, search intent alignment, content usefulness, and whether the page actually satisfies the query context.

2

VISIBLE_LOW_CTR

Review pages with observed visibility but CTR below 5%. Examine SERP presentation, title/snippet relevance, intent alignment, and whether the search result gives users a compelling reason to click.

3

WEAK_SEARCH_POSITION

For pages with average position above 20, inspect relevance, content depth, internal linking, topical coverage, and search intent before deciding whether a refresh is justified.

4

LOW_EVIDENCE / MONITOR_ONLY

When visibility or ranking evidence is weak, gather more evidence before allocating significant refresh effort. Lack of evidence should not automatically be interpreted as evidence of poor content quality.

Do not automate the recommendation: the queue should not automatically publish rewrites, delete or redirect pages, declare content low quality, change search intent, or treat the proxy label as ground truth.
