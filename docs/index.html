From Search Signals to Content Refresh Priorities

A public-safe research artifact testing whether observed search and engagement signals can help prioritize content for human review.

March 2026 development window331,437 content items55 clients~79M warehouse rows overall

Abstract

This study asks whether observed search and engagement signals can help prioritize content items for possible refresh review. Using the FlyRank pseudonymized warehouse and a March 2026 development window, I compared a simple impressions/clicks rule with a Random Forest using independent ranking and engagement features. The Week-4 baseline measured precision 0.768, recall 1.000, and F1 0.868, while the Week-5 Random Forest measured 0.309 precision, 0.693 recall, and 0.428 F1 on the same client-grouped test set. A controlled ML-09 validation produced high recall (0.973) but lower precision (0.122) and F1 (0.217), so the measured evidence does not show that the ML model outperforms the baseline. The resulting action playbook is therefore framed as directional decision-support for human review, not as proof that a page needs a refresh or that a refresh will improve performance.

Introduction / Problem statement

Content teams can have many pages competing for limited refresh time. A useful system should help answer a practical question: which content should a reviewer inspect first, and why?

The first version of this work used a transparent rule: pages with more than 100 impressions and no more than 1 click are possible opportunities because they have observed search visibility but very weak click activity. The research question was whether a machine-learning model using independent signals could provide stronger prioritization.

Data

The analysis uses the FlyRank pseudonymized warehouse release flyrank_pseudonymized_warehouse_release_v20260703, specifically the fact_content_daily_performance table. The warehouse contains roughly 79 million daily performance rows overall. Development and validation here use the March 2026 partition, containing 9,841,378 rows, 31 columns, 55 clients, and 331,437 content items.

March was used as a middle development window rather than the final month so a later period can remain conceptually sealed for future evaluation. Client names, URLs, raw search queries, and other private identifiers are excluded from this public artifact. Analytics-unavailable observations are not assumed to mean zero activity.

Methodology

Baseline

The Week-4 baseline flags a possible opportunity when gsc_impressions > 100 and gsc_clicks <= 1. This is a rule-based decision aid, not ground truth.

Model and label

The proxy label was (impressions > 100) AND (clicks <= 1) AND (GA4 sessions == 0), producing 388,493 positive and 9,452,885 negative observations. The positive class represents approximately 3.95% of the March development observations. The Random Forest used gsc_avg_position, ga4_pageviews, ga4_users, ga4_total_engagement_sec, and scroll_events. These features do not directly reproduce the label formula.

Validation

The train/test split was grouped by client: 44 training clients and 11 test clients, with zero overlap. Week 6 kept the full test set but used a 500,000-row training sample and a constrained Random Forest for memory-efficient revalidation. The leakage audit found no direct feature-to-label column overlap and the client-grouped split passed.

Results

On the same client-grouped test split, the transparent Week-4 baseline was stronger on all three reported metrics than the original Week-5 Random Forest. ML-09 increased recall but did so at a substantial precision cost, leaving its F1 below both the baseline and the original Week-5 model.

Limitations & honest framing

  • The refresh label is a constructed proxy, not human-confirmed ground truth.
  • March 2026 is one development window; relationships may change over time, clients, markets, and content types.
  • ML-09 was a controlled revalidation with a 500,000-row training sample, not an identical full-scale rerun.
  • Feature importance and association do not establish causal effects.
  • Measurement availability can change observed signals.
  • The queue is decision-support, not a production or autonomous content system.
  • Future work should validate recommendations against human-reviewed outcomes and later time periods.

Ranked recommendations

1. HIGH_VISIBILITY_LOW_CLICKS

Review pages with more than 100 impressions and no more than 1 click first. Inspect title, snippet, search intent, and content relevance.

2. VISIBLE_LOW_CTR

Review visible pages with CTR below 5%. Inspect SERP presentation and title/snippet relevance. This is a broader supporting signal than the baseline opportunity rule.

3. WEAK_SEARCH_POSITION

For pages with average position above 20, review relevance, content depth, internal linking, and search intent.

4. LOW_EVIDENCE / MONITOR_ONLY

Gather more evidence before investing in a refresh when visibility or ranking evidence is weak.

The Week-7 action playbook evaluated 331,437 content items and assigned 53,994 HIGH, 68,180 MEDIUM, and 209,263 LOW review priorities. Human review is required. Reviewers should check current intent, factual accuracy, business value, SERP context, and expected editing effort. Do not automatically publish rewrites, delete or redirect pages, declare content low quality, change search intent, or treat the proxy label as ground truth.

Reproducibility

The work is organized week by week in the repository:

  • GitHub repository — MLinternship
  • Week 4 — baseline score
  • Week 5 — model comparison
  • Week 7 — action playbook
  • Capstone — paper notebook

The documented experiment uses random_state=42, a client-grouped split, the stated feature/label definitions, and the Week-7 exported queue. The queue itself remains out of git because the repository's data guard blocks raw data exports.
